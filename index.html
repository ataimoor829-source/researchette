Why Researchette

Every medical student is told to “do research”. Almost nobody is shown how.

You need publications for your CV, for house job and residency applications, for FCPS and for going abroad. But supervisors are busy, and videos can’t read your draft. So students copy old synopses, guess at the format and wait months for replies.

I have written and published research as a medical student, and I have guided more than 50 students through their own papers to publication. The method is always the same: break the work into small steps, explain each step properly, let you write it, then correct it together. Researchette turns that method into a daily routine you can follow from anywhere.

We don’t write papers for you. You learn to write them yourself, with a mentor checking every step.

The core roadmap

Ten steps, in the order a real study is done.

Every member starts here. The next step unlocks once a mentor approves your current one, so nothing is built on a weak foundation.

  1. Research question

    Turn a clinical doubt into a focused question using PICO and FINER.

  2. Literature search

    PubMed, MeSH terms, Boolean operators and Google Scholar.

  3. Study design

    Pick the right design for your question and know the level of evidence it gives.

  4. Synopsis & ethics

    Write the proposal and get ERC/IRB approval and informed consent right.

  5. Sample size

    Calculate it with OpenEpi or the WHO calculator and choose a sampling technique.

  6. Proforma & data

    Build a validated questionnaire or proforma and collect clean data.

  7. Statistical analysis

    SPSS basics: descriptive stats, chi-square, t-test and p-values explained.

  8. Writing in IMRaD

    Introduction, methods, results and discussion, with tables journals accept.

  9. Referencing

    Vancouver style with Zotero or Mendeley, and avoiding plagiarism.

  10. Journal submission

    Choose an HEC-recognised or indexed journal, write the cover letter and answer reviewers.

Framework+

Write a research question with PICO

The format every clinical question starts from.

In [Population], does [Intervention/Exposure], compared with [Comparison], affect [Outcome]?

  • P: who exactly? Age, condition, setting.
  • I: the treatment, test or exposure.
  • C: what you compare it with.
  • O: something you can measure.

Then check it is Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical and Relevant (FINER).

Guide+

Search PubMed like a pro

Find the right papers in minutes.
  • Use MeSH terms: "Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2"[Mesh]
  • Combine synonyms with OR and concepts with AND: (metformin OR biguanide) AND HbA1c
  • Filter by article type, last 5 years and humans.
  • Use “Similar articles” and “Cited by” on a strong paper.
Cheat sheet+

Vancouver referencing

The style most medical journals use.

Journal article: Khan A, Ali S, Raza M. Title of article. J Pak Med Assoc. 2023;73(4):812-6.

  • Number references in the order you cite them: [1] or superscript¹.
  • List up to 6 authors, then “et al.”
  • Use NLM journal abbreviations.
Template+

Case report structure

Based on the CARE guidelines.
  1. Title with “case report” and the key diagnosis
  2. Abstract and 2–5 keywords
  3. Introduction: why this case matters
  4. Patient information and clinical findings
  5. Timeline of events
  6. Diagnostic assessment and treatment
  7. Follow-up and outcome
  8. Discussion and learning points
  9. Patient consent statement
Template+

Letter to the editor

A fast, realistic first publication.
  1. Open with “Dear Editor,”
  2. Cite the article you are responding to in the first line.
  3. Make one or two clear points: a gap, a limitation, or local data.
  4. Support each point with references.
  5. Close with a constructive conclusion.

Stay within the journal’s limits, usually 300–600 words and 5–10 references.

Roadmap+

Meta-analysis in 8 steps

What a PRISMA 2020 review involves.
  1. Write a PICO question and protocol.
  2. Register it on PROSPERO.
  3. Search at least 3 databases.
  4. Screen titles, abstracts, then full texts (two reviewers).
  5. Extract data into a sheet.
  6. Assess risk of bias (RoB 2 or Newcastle–Ottawa).
  7. Pool results and make a forest plot in RevMan.
  8. Report with the PRISMA flow diagram and checklist.

Questions

Frequently asked

Will you write my paper or synopsis for me?

No. We teach you to write it yourself and correct your work at every step. That’s how you learn, and it keeps your work original and ethical.

I’m in first year and have never done research. Can I join?

Yes. The roadmap starts from zero. Many students begin with a letter to the editor or a case report before a full study.

Will I get published?

We guide you through choosing a suitable journal, formatting, the cover letter and replying to reviewers. The final decision is always the journal’s, but our mentored students have published their work.

How much time do I need each day?

Most tasks take 30 to 60 minutes, so it fits around classes and wards. The next step unlocks once your current one is approved.

How quickly is my work reviewed?

Mentors aim to review each submission within 24 to 48 hours.
